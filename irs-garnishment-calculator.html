Wage Garnishment Calculator

Estimate how much of your paycheck a general creditor can take under federal law—and see why an IRS levy follows different rules.

Before you start

This tool estimates the general federal limit on wage garnishment under Title III of the Consumer Credit Protection Act (15 U.S.C. § 1673). It applies to most ordinary creditor and judgment garnishments. IRS tax levies do not use this formula—see the section below. Child support and student-loan garnishments also follow their own rules.

Estimate your limit

“Disposable earnings” means your take-home pay after legally required deductions (taxes, Social Security, Medicare)—not after voluntary deductions like retirement or insurance. Minimum wage defaults to the federal rate; enter your state’s rate if it is higher.

Your estimate

Enter your pay details and select Estimate to see the maximum a general creditor could garnish this pay period.

This is a general estimate for education only—not legal or tax advice, and not a prediction about any specific case. Your actual protection may be greater under state law.

How the general federal limit works

For most creditor garnishments, federal law caps the amount taken from each paycheck at the lesser of:

  • 25% of your disposable earnings for that pay period, or
  • the amount by which your disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage (scaled to your pay period).

If your disposable earnings are at or below that protected floor, wages generally cannot be garnished at all for an ordinary debt. Many states protect more of your pay than the federal minimum.

The per-pay-period multiples this tool uses (30× weekly, 60× bi-weekly, 65× semi-monthly, 130× monthly) are set by 29 CFR § 870.10.

Why an IRS wage levy is different

An IRS levy on wages is not limited by the 25% rule above. Instead, the IRS lets you keep an exempt amount set by Internal Revenue Code § 6334 and published each year in IRS Publication 1494. That exempt amount depends on your filing status, pay period, and number of dependents—the rest of your pay can be levied.

Because the Publication 1494 tables change every year, this calculator does not estimate an IRS levy figure. To pin down what the IRS can actually take—and to stop or release a levy—work from the current-year table with a professional who can review your notice. Florida also provides a strong exemption for the wages of a head of family under Fla. Stat. § 222.11, which can protect earnings that federal law alone would not.
